# experiments/findings.md

Answers to the five M&S goals, generated by `analysis/findings.py` from the NetLogo BehaviorSpace results (`experiments/E*_results.csv`, per-run summary in `summary_runs.csv`) and the thresholds in `thresholds.csv`. Net benefit is dollars per household relative to the no-data-center baseline (definition in `notes/model_spec.md`); cumulative values are 20-year undiscounted sums. All results are conditional on the ASSUMPTION parameters in `data/parameters.csv`.

## Base case (E1, Project Sail, 900 MW)

- 90% of 900 MW energized by year 9.25; 900 MW at year 20.
- Net data center revenue 100.3 $M/yr at year 20; cumulative net fiscal impact 812 $M.
- Electricity rate index peaks at 1.0012 (data centers pay); peak water stress 0.689 (baseline 0.675); housing cost 1.7% above baseline at year 20.
- Opposition peaks at 0.51 during construction and settles to 0.35 (initial 0.40); permitting factor falls to 0.76.
- Cumulative net benefit 2763 $/household (low point -1276 during build-out; positive for good from year 15.1); 557 $/household/yr at year 20.

NetLogo check at the thresholds (cumulative net benefit should be about 0 $/household): evaporative at 1458 MW -> -0.00; ql_cost = 2024 at Sail -> 0.00; k_prox = 0.03549 at Sail -> -0.00.

Reading:
- Under base assumptions there is no size threshold: every term scales roughly with MW, so net benefit is positive at every size with closed-loop cooling and data-center-paid grid upgrades. Size changes the magnitude, not the sign.
- Evaporative cooling creates a real size threshold (1458 MW; 1188 MW with a drought): above it, water stress exceeds 1 and shortage costs and opposition outweigh tax revenue.
- Socializing grid costs on ratepayers gives a threshold of 3687 MW; with evaporative cooling as well, 1257 MW; the worst policy combination is a net cost above 530 MW (1063 MW without drought).
- The judgement parameters decide the sign at any size: see the ql_cost, k_prox and O0 break-evens above. They sit inside their plausible ranges, so the honest answer is that Sail is a modest net benefit only if its quality-of-life and housing costs are at or below the assumed values.

- Cooling matters most at large size: evaporative cooling at 3500 MW lifts peak water stress from 0.73 to 1.78 (2.30 with drought) and turns net benefit from 11316 to -23364 $/household. At Sail's size it lowers net benefit by 843 $/household but stays positive.

- With feedback on, opposition delays 90% energization by 0.12 to 3.12 years and lowers year-20 net fiscal impact by 0.3% to 12.4%. The effect grows with size and with slower contracting (longer construction keeps opposition high).
- Opposition slows but never stalls build-out in these runs: the lowest permitting factor is 0.37 (3500 MW, t-sign 3), and every run reaches at least 99% of its target by year 20. A stall (permit factor 0) needs opposition above 1/s_perm = 0.83, which only the forced-opposition extreme test reaches; the closest policy case is the worst combination at 3500 MW, where the permit factor falls to 0.12.
- The relief loop (R1) pulls opposition below its starting value at large sizes (year-20 opposition 0.16 at 3500 MW), which is why bigger campuses can face less opposition after construction ends.

## Goal 5: validation

See `experiments/validation_log.md`: units audit clean; 10/10 extreme-condition tests pass in NetLogo; year-20 outputs change by at most 0.40% from dt 0.125 to 0.0625; model revenue share (4.8% of the county budget at year 10, 7.8% at year 20) sits inside Douglas County's observed 3-8%; closed-loop and evaporative water shares bracket Newton County's 10%; sensitivity ranks k_prox, ql_cost, rev_per_MW and O0 as the most influential parameters; net benefit is positive in about half of a Latin hypercube over the top ASSUMPTION parameters.
